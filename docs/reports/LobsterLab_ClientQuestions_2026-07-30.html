3. The guest reviews on the site

What I need to know: did anyone ask permission from the guests whose reviews you sent?

The situation: your brief included six lovely reviews from Google and Yelp, each with the reviewer’s full name. I have published the quotes but changed the credits to non-identifying for now — they read “Google reviewer, Carlsbad” instead of the person’s name. The real names are stored safely and can go back immediately with the go-ahead.

Why: Yelp’s terms restrict republishing review content outside their own display tools, and they enforce it. Google reviews belong to whoever wrote them, not to the business. Separately, California Civil Code § 3344 covers using a person’s name in advertising without consent, with statutory damages starting at $750 per instance — six named reviewers is six separate exposures.

The clean fix: use Yelp’s and Google’s official review widgets, which are licensed for exactly this. They look good, update themselves, and remove the problem. Happy to install them — I just need to know you are comfortable with that, or that you have written permission from each reviewer.

5. Tiger Hospitality Group’s total annual revenue

What I need: roughly, is THG’s total gross annual revenue across all concepts above or below US$25 million?

Why I am asking something so blunt: California’s privacy law (CCPA/CPRA) only applies above certain thresholds, and $25M gross annual revenue is one of them. Critically, that threshold is measured for the whole company, not per brand — five Lobster Lab units alone would not trigger it, but if THG’s group total is above $25M it applies to the group, including this website.

The difference is real work: above it, the site needs a “Your Privacy Choices” link plus a documented process for customers to request access to or deletion of their data. Below it, what we have is sufficient. Guessing wrong either way is bad — over-building wastes your money, under-building leaves a gap. A simple “above” or “below” is all I need, not the figure.

Also worth telling your attorney — all good news:

•  A Privacy Policy and Terms of Use are now published. The privacy policy became legally required the moment the catering form started collecting names, phone numbers and emails from California residents (Cal. Bus. & Prof. Code § 22575). There was none before — that gap is now closed.

•  The catering form has an optional, unchecked consent box for marketing texts. Agreeing to be contacted about a catering request is not the same as agreeing to marketing, and under the TCPA marketing texts need separate express written consent. Keeping them separate protects you.

•  Calorie labelling is not required for you. Federal rules start at 20 or more locations; at five you are exempt. I built the menu data so nutrition can be added later without a rebuild.

•  Please double-check your Proposition 65 signage at each location covers seafood (mercury) and fried items (acrylamide). That is an on-premises posting requirement, not a website one, but it came up while reviewing the menus.

•  There is an accessibility statement and the site is built to WCAG 2.1 AA. I would recommend a formal accessibility audit within a few months of launch — California sees a high volume of website accessibility claims against restaurants, and a dated audit plus a record of fixes is the practical protection.
